## Read this first

Everyone wants to know how smart AI is. The honest answer is that "how smart" is the wrong question. It is five questions.

Can it reason? Can it remember and learn? Can it improve itself? Can it work inside an organization that checks its work? And, the one nobody asks: **what can it actually change in the world?**

This book is about the last question. It says the road from today's chatbots to a civilization that captures the energy of its star runs through **five circles**. Each circle is a kind of thing AI must learn to change: software, then hardware, then physical machines, then living things, then infrastructure in space. Each circle has a few hard problems that decide whether we get through it. This free edition names them all.

You do not need a technical background to read it. If a word is new, it is explained where it appears.

## 1. What "AI level" means

A chess program is superhuman at chess and cannot make coffee. A language model can write a legal brief and forgets everything when the conversation ends. "Smart" hides these differences. We measure six things instead:

- **Cognition** — can it solve the problem in front of it?
- **Individual growth** — does it remember across sessions, learn from what it got wrong, and get better over time?
- **Organization** — can a group of AIs (and people) work together with someone checking the work?
- **Task difficulty** — how hard a job can you hand it?
- **Human help** — how much thinking does a person still have to supply?
- **Self-awareness** — does it know what it knows, and admit what it cannot do?

Two rules make these measurements honest.

**A confident wrong answer costs more than "I can't."** Most AI tests give a point for a right answer and nothing for a wrong one, so guessing is free. We charge for it. An AI that hands you wrong work as if it were finished is worse than one that says it is stuck. This one rule changes which AI looks best.

**The AI and its scaffolding are measured together.** The same model gives different results depending on the tools, memory and checking wrapped around it. A score is a score of the pair. We say which pair.

And one honest fact: measured this way, today's language models are at the entrance. They reason well, they remember nothing after the conversation ends, and every step that matters — deciding whether the work is right, deciding whether to use it — is still done by a person. That is not an insult. It is where the road begins.

## 2. The five circles

The idea is simple. Getting smarter is one thing. Being able to **change something and check that the change worked** is another. Every real improvement, in any field, has four steps:

1. **Identify** what to change.
2. **Implement** the change.
3. **Validate** — check that it worked, honestly.
4. **Deploy** — put it into use.

A circle is defined by *what kind of thing* the loop can change. There are five.

**Circle I — Software.** The AI improves software, including its own. Cheap to try, fast to check.

**Circle II — Hardware.** The AI helps design and make the chips it runs on. Designing is fast. Making is a building, a supply chain and physics.

**Circle III — Machines and matter.** Robots and factories that build, repair and copy themselves from raw materials.

**Circle IV — Life.** Discoveries about biology that become real treatments — for cancer, aging, damaged organs, the brain. This circle runs alongside the others; it does not wait for them.

**Circle V+ — Space.** Industry in orbit that grows by itself, until it captures a meaningful fraction of the Sun's output. That is what a *Type II civilization* means, and a *Dyson swarm* — thousands of collectors orbiting the star — is what it would look like.

Two facts about the circles shape everything else.

**Checking and deploying are never done by the thing being improved.** A student cannot grade their own exam. A system that approves its own changes has proved nothing. So in every circle, two of the four steps need an outside party — a referee. As far as anyone has shown, that referee is still a human in every circle. Getting a trustworthy non-human referee is the first grand problem, and it comes up again in every circle after.

**Smarter alone does not get you out.** Designing a chip, a machine or a solar collector is ordinary engineering thinking. The hard part of the outer circles is everything *after* the design: making it, testing it in the world, keeping it running. Those are problems of organization, materials and time, not of raw intelligence. The outer circles are won by organizations, not by a single brilliant mind.

## 3. The grand problems, circle by circle

Eighteen of these problems are already registered as open challenges at **physicsworldmodel.org/frontier**, each with a precise statement of what "solved" would mean and a way to earn credit for progress. They are marked ★. The rest are from the full book.

### Circle I — Software

- **A referee that cannot be fooled.** Tests the AI never sees, checks it cannot rewrite, and a rule that punishes confident wrong answers. Everything else depends on this.
- **Memory that survives.** Remembering across restarts, knowing where each fact came from, and learning from verified feedback — not just storing text.
- **Improving itself, provably.** Changing its own reasoning in a way that an outside check confirms made it better, without breaking what already worked.
- **Organizations of AIs.** Groups with real division of labour, where the one proposing a change is never the one approving it.
- **A ruler that does not move.** A way of scoring AI progress that means the same thing next year as it does today.
- ★ **Four problems in physics that can be attacked entirely in computation:** the geometry of spacetime from quantum entanglement (*quantum gravity*); what dark matter is, from how galaxies spin; whether dark energy is constant or changing; and whether black holes really destroy information. These are the cleanest tests of whether an AI can *discover* something and be checked by data it cannot see.

### Circle II — Hardware

- **The yield loop.** Getting a chip factory to learn from its own mistakes at the speed of physics, not the speed of code.
- **Running on what you made.** A system whose new hardware actually goes into its own computers.
- **More work per watt.** Energy and cooling, not cleverness, are the ceiling.
- ★ **Quantum computers that stay correct** as they get bigger; ★ **control pulses** precise enough to run them; ★ **compilers** that fit a quantum program onto real hardware; ★ **single molecules as electronic parts**; ★ **a room-temperature superconductor**, which would change the energy story in every circle.

### Circle III — Machines and matter

- **A machine that builds a working copy of itself** — and lists honestly every part it had to import.
- **Robots that handle what nobody planned for**, and repair what breaks.
- **From rock to machine**: mining, refining and manufacturing as one connected system.
- **Checking with instruments the machine does not control.**
- ★ **Building matter one atom at a time**; ★ **DNA that folds itself into designed shapes** — reproduction at the smallest scale.

### Circle IV — Life

- **Finishing the loop through the clinic.** Biology has a clock that cannot be hurried: a treatment must be tested in real bodies over real time, with consent and accountability. The discovery loop can run fast inside a validation loop that cannot.
- **Labs that learn.** Automated experiments whose results genuinely change what the lab tries next.
- ★ **Reversing aging** at the level of the cell; ★ **slowing the rate of aging** itself; ★ **cancer as an evolving population** that can be kept under control instead of driven to resistance; ★ **regeneration instead of scar**; ★ **the protein clumping behind Alzheimer's and Parkinson's**; ★ **drugs delivered only where they are needed.**

### Circle V+ — Space

- **Industry in orbit that grows faster than it wears out.** Not one collector: a loop that builds collectors from the energy it collects.
- **Supply chains off Earth.** Mining and refining where the materials are.
- **Repair over decades**, without anyone flying up to fix it.
- **Organization across light-minutes.** A swarm the size of Earth's orbit is sixteen light-minutes across. No single mind, human or machine, can run it in real time. This is the deepest problem in the book, and it is a problem of organization, not intelligence. ★ **Quantum networks** that carry trust across distance are one piece of it.

## 4. Which circle is it now?

Circle I. Not because the others are unimportant — Circle IV alone would justify a lifetime — but because Circle I is the only one whose loop is close to closing, and its first grand problem, the referee, is the one every other circle inherits.

The cheapest useful thing anyone can do this year is in the software circle: build a test an AI cannot game, run a memory experiment with the memory switched off as the control, or take one of the four physics problems and build the benchmark that would let an AI be checked against it.

The rest of the circles can start now in parallel. They just cannot finish first.

---

## 5. What you can do

Pick one problem. Any of the forty. Then:

1. **Find the real constraint.** Not the exciting part — the part that is actually stopping progress.
2. **Build the smallest thing that would show progress**, and decide in advance what would count as failure.
3. **Get someone who is not you to check it.**
4. **Make it available for the next person.**

If your problem is one of the eighteen marked ★, the benchmark that would measure it does not exist yet. Building it is the next step, and physicsworldmodel.org pays for that step. If your problem is not on the list, register it there so it can be.
